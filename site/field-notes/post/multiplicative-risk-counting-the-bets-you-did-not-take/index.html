Multiplicative Risk: Counting the Bets You Did Not Take

Caprae Capital & Kevin Hong Dec 16, 2025 Source: capraecapitalpartners.substack.com ↗
Read the original at capraecapitalpartners.substack.com ↗

Most firms count what they own. Few count what they omitted. In private capital, opportunity cost is the invisible risk: the deals never screened, the emails ignored, the “close, but no” that quietly rewrites a fund’s destiny. Returns aren’t purely additive from yeses; they’re multiplicative, shaped by disciplined subtraction.

The Invisible Ledger

The math is unforgiving. Cambridge Associates’ review of 27,000 venture financings found that less than 6% of deals drove over 60% of returns. Missing just one of those outliers rewrites a fund’s trajectory.1 Bain’s 2024 Global PE Report echoes the same: the spread between top-quartile and median funds wasn’t volume of deals, it was selection.2

In other words, opportunity cost isn’t abstract philosophy. It is a quantifiable subtraction that silently accumulates. Think of it this way: if each “no” carries a hidden optionality, then a fund’s return profile is not the sum of its choices but the product of its omissions. That’s multiplicative risk.

Bessemer Venture Partners: The Anti-Portfolio That Speaks Louder Than the Portfolio

Bessemer Venture Partners publicly lists its “Anti-Portfolio”, the iconic companies they passed on: Apple, Google, Airbnb, Facebook, Tesla, and more.3

To outsiders, it’s mea culpa. To disciplined investors, it’s measurement: a running ledger of lost optionality that keeps selection rigor honest. Counting “missed bets” is not self-flagellation; it’s governance.

Subtraction as Conviction

At Caprae, subtraction is strategy. We deliberately opt out of most pipelines to protect attention for the few compounding bets that matter. That means killing attractive stories when the system is unconvincing. Our mystique doesn’t come from volume; it comes from auditable judgment, the ability to explain why a pass preserved dry powder for a rarer asymmetry tomorrow. In a power-law world, more “yes” can mean less return; more “no” can mean higher signal density.

Psychological Gut Checks

If your reporting celebrates only deployed capital and realized IRR, you’re blind to the counterfactual ledger. The market won’t show you the shadow portfolio you declined, but your results will. The sober question is not “How many deals did we win?” It’s: “Which omissions made our winners possible, and which passes will we regret?” Count both ledgers or multiplicative risk will count you.
